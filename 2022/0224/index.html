Reflections on the Passing of a 28-Year-Old ByteDance Engineer

February 24, 2022 life views:

Earlier, rumors circulated online that a young ByteDance employee, Mr. Wu, had passed away. A notice on ByteDance’s internal network stated that the company had been informed by the hospital that Mr. Wu had unfortunately passed away. The sequence of events was roughly as follows: At 6:00 p.m. on February 21, 28-year-old Mr. Wu entered ByteDance’s gym. After exercising for about an hour, he began to feel dizzy, and his condition worsened. Around 7:30 p.m. that evening, emergency medical personnel arrived on the scene, treated him, and transported him to the hospital in an ambulance. At 12:30 a.m. on February 23, the hospital reported that the employee was in critical condition. Shortly thereafter, Mr. Wu passed away.

Speaking of this, I, too, initially applied to a computer-related major out of curiosity, which led me to a school I wasn’t particularly satisfied with afterward. At 28, he could be called a “senior”—a few years older than me—but his life was still so young! Senior Wu held a degree from a prestigious university and had long aspired to work in the IT industry. After graduating, he joined ByteDance and worked diligently; he got married, bought a home, published a book, and just a few months ago, his wife gave birth to their child. It’s hard to imagine that such a young senior—who should have gone on to lead a happier and more fulfilling life—would be taken from us so suddenly by such a cruel reality.

Judging from Senior Wu’s social media accounts and current reports, he was a dreamer, lively and cheerful, and highly capable—he even owned a home, albeit with a mortgage. Despite the hospital’s best efforts to save him, they were unable to save his life. Wu’s passing has placed immense pressure on his wife. She plans to sell their home and return to her small county town to raise their child with her mother’s help.

Regarding this news, I feel saddened and a sense of regret over Wu’s passing—regret that he was someone who deserved better from life, yet met such a tragic end; I also feel lost as a software engineering major myself, as I’m torn between finding a job and preparing for graduate school; I’m now questioning whether I should continue studying computer science or enter a related field. I also feel a mix of concern and relief for my fellow students majoring in computer-related fields or those about to enter the industry—relief that we still have our health and our lives, and relief that we still have some choices.

Senior Wu’s passing is not an isolated case; another 26-year-old has also trended on social media. A designer at a Shanghai-based design firm reportedly died suddenly in his rented apartment early on the 15th, allegedly due to excessive overtime. The news of these two young people’s deaths coming so close together has had a particularly profound impact. Is this really the reality of life? Everyone knows that overtime is widespread in the IT industry, where work pressure and life pressures are both intense, and most professionals live in big cities. It’s understood that companies like ByteDance prefer highly self-disciplined individuals; they generally offer generous benefits, including free meals and beverages, a free gym, social insurance, and housing fund contributions. In such an environment, it’s hard not to strive for advancement, and even when you get home, you’re constantly studying.

In short, the IT industry can be summed up in one word: “cutthroat competition”! Due to a widespread societal perception that the industry offers high salaries, the competition isn’t limited to the job search—it has even spilled over into the college entrance exams and graduate school applications. Everyone is rushing to major in computer-related fields, even switching majors, all with the sole goal of securing a high-paying job in the future. Yet, as the number of applicants continues to rise, even expanded enrollment quotas can’t keep up with demand, making the competition increasingly fierce. It’s not that other industries aren’t competitive—it’s just that the computer science field has become so intensely cutthroat that it’s off the charts! Regardless of whether a major is a good fit or if one is actually capable of doing the work, people are flooding into this trend—especially since the rise of artificial intelligence.

At the same time, working at a major company like ByteDance today allows you to freely enjoy various generous benefits, but you’ll also feel a kind of “restricted freedom.” Despite flexible work arrangements, employees must complete various tasks; working alongside outstanding colleagues can help you excel, but you also have to endure the hard work and exhaustion. This isn’t to say people shouldn’t strive for success, but rather to consider what it brings them. If we overlook long-term benefits and the harm caused by forced overwork, even the strongest giants will eventually fall.

When I saw the news about Senior Wu, I was deeply moved and wondered why such misfortune had befallen him. While it’s difficult to determine whether his sudden death was linked to overtime work, we should reflect on how to keep both the tech giant ByteDance and its employees thriving amidst such intense life pressures.
